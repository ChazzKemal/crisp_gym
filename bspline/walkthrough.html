crisp_gym · b-spline action representation

Knots Instead
of Waypoints

A policy usually predicts a list of future poses — one row per control tick, whether the robot is inching toward a grasp or sweeping across the table. This walks through the alternative, on one real episode from our own pick-and-place recordings: fit a spline to the whole demonstration, then predict a slice of that.

    01The recording

    What one episode actually is

    Every frame of a crisp_gym cart7 recording is seven numbers: an end-effector position, an axis-angle rotation, and a gripper command. Twenty frames a second, for as long as the demonstration runs.

    Below is episode 58 — two full pick-and-place cycles. The shape to notice is how uneven it is. Long, lazy transits where the arm is just moving across the workspace, then tight clusters of activity around each grasp and release. A fixed-rate list of waypoints spends exactly as many numbers on the boring parts as on the delicate ones.

    02The fit

    One spline for the whole demonstration

    Fit a single cubic B-spline through all ten policy dimensions at once, and keep adding knots until the worst error on any frame, in any dimension, drops below a tolerance.

    That last part is what makes this interesting. The knot placement is not uniform — the fitter puts knots where the trajectory needs them. Watch the tick strip under the curve: knots crowd together at the grasps and thin out across the transits, which is precisely the unevenness that a fixed-rate waypoint list has no way to exploit.

    03The slice

    A chunk is a window of that spline

    Take a run of consecutive knots — and, crucially, the control points carrying the same indices. That pair is a valid B-spline in its own right.

    B-splines have local support: each control point only influences the curve over a short span of knots. So a window of the knot vector plus the matching control points reproduces the global curve exactly over the window's interior — no refitting, no approximation. That interior is what gets predicted and executed; the extra knots on each end are just the overhang the cubic basis needs.

    04The chunk, up close

    Drag through the episode

    This is the whole method in one picture. Move the scrubber to pick a frame; the panel shows the chunk that frame is assigned, the curve it encodes, and the waypoints you get by decoding it.

    What the policy actually emits

    Stored form is a dense matrix: one row per knot, one column for the knot value and one for each action dimension. Flattened, that is numbers — a single vector for the network to regress.

    column 0 — knots
    Where in time, measured in frames from now, each span begins. Must come out non-decreasing.
    columns 1–10 — control points
    Position, a six-number continuous rotation, and the gripper. Not poses on the path — they pull the curve toward them.
    05The assignment

    Every frame gets one

    Each frame is handed the last chunk whose window has not yet opened, with the knot column shifted so it reads as time-from-now. Neighbouring frames usually share a chunk and differ by a single subtracted frame.

    06The decode

    Back to something the arm can run

    Sample the chunk's curve at as many points as you want across its valid span, convert the rotation back to axis-angle, and you have ordinary end-effector waypoints again.

    Two properties fall out. First, how many waypoints you take is a decode-time choice, not a property of the prediction — ask for 8 or 64 and you are sampling the same curve. Second, how fast you run them is also a decode-time choice: the knots say the span is worth some number of frames, and nothing forces you to spend that many. Halve it and the arm traces the identical path in half the time.

    07The tuning

    Why the tolerance matters more than anything

    Tolerance sets knot density, knot density sets how much time a chunk buys. Too tight and the spline needs nearly a knot per frame — at which point you have reinvented the waypoint list, with extra steps.

    The reference implementation ships max_error = 0.002. On their 10 Hz data that is fine; on ours it produces almost no compression. The sweep below is this episode, measured.

    max_errorknots / frameposition errorchunk covers

    We settled on 0.01: positions stay inside a few millimetres while a chunk still reaches roughly two seconds ahead. And chunk_size = 20 rather than the reference's 10, because our recordings run at 20 Hz where theirs run at 10 — twice the frames for the same wall-clock horizon.

    08The training run

    What went into the model

    The whole 70-episode merged_act_finetune_20260528 dataset was converted this way: the action column becomes a flat -number vector, observations and videos untouched. An ACT policy then predicts one such vector per observation — a chunk size of 1 at the policy level, because the time axis already lives inside the action.

    Verified before training, by decoding stored actions and comparing against the source recording across the full dataset: within 5.4 mm and 0.78°, median chunk span 45 frames. For comparison, the ACT baseline it is measured against predicts 700 numbers to cover 100 frames.

    Two rough edges, reproduced on purpose

    The last waypoint of a padded chunk is garbage. When a window runs past the end of the knot vector the reference pads by repeating the final knot — so at exactly the span's end every basis function has already closed and the curve evaluates to zero. Only the final chunk of an episode is affected; the decoder drops that sample.

    The last few frames of each episode look backwards. Once the chunks run out, the final one keeps being reassigned with a growing shift, so its window starts before the current frame. Roughly the last 5% of each episode; the decoder clamps to zero.

    Both are upstream behaviour rather than porting mistakes, and both are pinned by tests so a later refactor cannot quietly change them.
